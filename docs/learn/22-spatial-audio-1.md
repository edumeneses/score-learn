# Lesson 22: Spatial audio, Part I: speaker layouts and panning

{% include lesson_meta.html %}

> **Before this lesson** finish [Lesson 21]({{ site.baseurl }}/learn/21-effects-and-plugins.html), particularly the polyphony section, which this lesson assumes.
>
> **You will need** a mono sound file and headphones, since no multichannel interface is required.
>
> **You will build** a sound that travels around a four-speaker layout, monitored as a stereo fold-down on headphones.

## Why this matters

Spatial audio is the reason many people come to this software, and it is where the indifference of *score* to channel count pays off, because ports carry as many channels as arrive and a dome with ninety speakers is therefore not a special case.

However, spatial audio is also a subject where the tooling can obscure the ideas, so this lesson insists on the decomposition below, which separates the description of a scene into four parts and leaves the audio work to a single further object. Once you can name which part you are missing, every spatial problem becomes tractable, whether you are using the built-in algorithms, a Faust library, a plug-in suite, or driving an external spatialisation server.

## Concepts

### The four parts of a spatial scene

A spatial scene is described by four parts, and a fifth object performs the audio work. Much confusion comes from conflating the four descriptive parts:

1. a **source**, usually mono, such as a sound file, a synthesiser, or a live input;
2. a **layout**, which records where the loudspeakers physically are, as 2D or 3D coordinates;
3. a **trajectory**, which states where the source should be over time;
4. an **algorithm**, which computes, from the layout and the position, how much signal each speaker gets.

The object that does the audio work is the **spatialisation matrix**, which takes the source and the per-speaker weights the algorithm produced and outputs the multichannel signal. In other words, the layout, the trajectory, and the algorithm describe the scene without carrying audio, whereas the matrix is where the source's audio becomes a multichannel signal.

### Speaker layouts

A layout can be placed by hand or generated by expression. Positions can come from a multi-cursor object whose points you place by hand, or be generated by expression: a small array generator returning `[cos(2*pi*i/n), sin(2*pi*i/n)]` lays out any number of speakers on a circle, and an expression generator can return coordinates literally. Additionally, a point view object draws them, so that you can see what you declared.

### Trajectories

A trajectory can be generated or drawn. A **path generator** produces movement along a line, a circle, or a spiral, and loops it, whereas a **2D spline** lets you draw a path by hand and is the right tool when the movement is composed and not generated. Both output a position, which is what the algorithm expects as its source input.

### DBAP and GBAP panning

DBAP (distance-based amplitude panning) is the default algorithm, and GBAP (grid-based amplitude panning) suits regular grids. **Distance-based amplitude panning** computes levels from distance and copes with irregular layouts, speakers at different heights, and arrays from two to a hundred, which is why it is the default choice. **Grid-based amplitude panning** suits regular grids and, usefully, cascades, so that one instance can spatialise at the level of a building and others within each room. Furthermore, **vector-based panning** and **ambisonics** are available through Faust libraries and plug-ins, whereas neither ships as a built-in process.

### Faust libraries, plug-ins, and SpatGRIS

The Faust libraries and hosted plug-ins extend the built-in algorithms. The Faust standard library provides a professional circular spatialiser, `sp.spat`, developed by Laurent Pottier, which assumes a ring of speakers around the listener; its speaker count is a number in the script that you can edit and recompile. The abclib library, installable through the package manager, adds 2D ambisonics up to high orders with encoders, decoders, and geometric tools. Plug-in suites such as SPARTA, IEM, and Ambix can be hosted per Lesson 21, and *score* can act purely as a controller for an external server such as SpatGRIS over OSC (Open Sound Control).

### Fold-down and binaural monitoring

Monitoring without the rig means choosing between a fold-down and a binaural simulation. Few people author in the room they will perform in, so you either fold the multichannel output down to stereo for a rough check, or use an HRTF (head-related transfer function) plug-in to simulate the layout over headphones. Both approaches approximate a room you have not yet heard. Nevertheless, either is better than authoring blind.

## Walkthrough: a sound that circles four speakers

{: .note }
> A figure for this lesson is pending, because the scene is assembled in the nodal view and needs audio content, so it requires interaction and media that this course does not ship; see `checks/22-spatial-audio-1.md`.

1. **Switch to the nodal view**, where this lesson is built from beginning to end.
2. **Add the source** by dropping a mono sound file and setting it to loop in its inspector, so that you have continuous material to listen to.
3. **Declare the layout** by adding a multi-cursor object and placing four points in a square, or by generating them with an expression that returns four positions on a circle, and add a point view to see them.
4. **Add the trajectory** as a path generator set to a circle, whose output is a position over time.
5. **Add the algorithm** as a DBAP process, and cable the layout into its layout inlet and the trajectory into its source inlet.
6. **Add the matrix** as a spatialisation matrix with four outputs, and cable the DBAP output into its data inlet and the sound file's audio into its audio inlet.
7. **Play**, and the sound should move; if your interface has four outputs, route them, and if not, continue to the next step.
8. **Fold down to stereo** so that you can hear it on headphones, either by summing the four channels into two or by inserting an HRTF plug-in configured for your layout; this is a check, whereas the deliverable is the four-channel scene.
9. **Change the layout without touching the piece** by moving one speaker's coordinate and listening again; no other part of the patch changes, which is the point of separating layout from trajectory.
10. **Change the number of speakers** by regenerating the layout with eight points and setting the matrix to eight outputs, while the trajectory and the algorithm stay untouched.
11. **Automate the trajectory's parameters** by right-clicking the path generator's rate or radius port and creating an automation, so that the movement evolves over the piece and does not circle forever at one speed.
12. **Write the layout down**, with speaker numbers, physical positions, and the output channel each corresponds to, because this document is what makes the piece installable by somebody else.

## Authoring for a room you are not in

The difference between a piece that transfers to the venue and one that has to be rebuilt there comes down to three decisions made while authoring.

**A parameterised layout makes a change of venue a change of two numbers.** If the speaker positions are generated from a count and a radius, changing venue is changing two numbers. In contrast, if they are twelve hand-placed points, changing venue is an afternoon.

**A trajectory composed in normalised space survives a room of a different size.** A path expressed relative to the layout's extent survives a bigger or smaller room, whereas one expressed in metres does not.

**Every piece with a specific layout needs a documented answer for the venue that has half of it**, stating which speakers are essential and what the fold-down is. Deciding this while authoring is far cheaper than deciding it during a load-in, and DBAP's tolerance of irregular layouts is what makes a graceful answer possible.

## Object-based, channel-based, and controller

A spatial project can be organised in three ways, object-based, channel-based, and controller, and knowing which of them you are in prevents most confusion.

**An object-based project, which this lesson builds, treats the layout as data.** Sources have positions, an algorithm computes speaker levels, and the layout is data, so the piece adapts to any room, which is why this organisation is the right default for touring work.

**A channel-based project routes material that is already multichannel.** In contrast to the object-based case, the material was mixed for a specific layout, and the job of *score* is to route it, which is simple and reliable and tied to the layout it was mixed for; ambisonic material is a variant, which needs a decoder for the target layout.

**A controller project leaves spatialisation to another application.** The spatialisation happens in a dedicated server such as SpatGRIS, and *score* sends it positions over OSC, which is right when the venue already runs that server or when the piece needs an algorithm that *score* does not have.

These organisations combine, so that a piece can have object-based sources, a channel-based bed, and a controlled external layer at once. However, doing the same job twice causes trouble, for example spatialising a source that is already positioned by the server, which produces movement that no one at the console can explain.

## Common mistakes

- **Conflating trajectory and layout** confuses two separate inputs to the algorithm, and keeping them separate is what makes the piece portable.
- **Forgetting the matrix** leaves the scene silent, because DBAP produces weights and not audio, and without the matrix no signal is spatialised.
- **Expecting the algorithm to know your channel order** fails because speaker one in your layout is whichever output you cabled it to, which is why you write it down.
- **Authoring on stereo speakers with no fold-down** means discovering in the room that the movement is inaudible.
- **Choosing ambisonics because it sounds more advanced** ignores that, for an irregular room, DBAP will usually do a better job with less machinery.
- **Composing a trajectory in absolute units** ties the movement to the dimensions of one room, so that the next venue requires the trajectory to be redrawn.
- **Not looping the source** while tuning means spending the session restarting playback.

## Exercise

Build the four-speaker circling scene, then change it three ways without touching the trajectory: to eight speakers on a circle, to four speakers in an irregular arrangement with one much further away, and to a layout with two speakers raised above the others. Listen to each variation through your fold-down and write one sentence on what changed perceptually.

**Success criterion:** all three variations work with the trajectory and algorithm untouched, and your written layout document is complete enough that someone else could patch your outputs. If a variation required editing the trajectory, your layout and movement are still entangled.
