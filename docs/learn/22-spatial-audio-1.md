# Lesson 22: Spatial audio, Part I

{% include lesson_meta.html %}

> **Before this lesson** finish [Lesson 21]({{ site.baseurl }}/learn/21-effects-and-plugins.html), particularly the polyphony section.
>
> **You will need** a mono sound file and headphones. No multichannel interface is required.
>
> **You will build** a sound that travels around a four-speaker layout, monitored as a stereo fold-down.

## Why this matters

Spatial audio is the reason many people come to this software, and it is where *score*'s indifference to channel count pays off: ports carry as many channels as arrive, so a dome with ninety speakers is not a special case.

It is also a subject where the tooling can obscure the ideas. So this lesson insists on the four-part decomposition below. Once you can name which of the four parts you are missing, every spatial problem becomes tractable, whether you are using the built-in algorithms, a Faust library, a plug-in suite, or driving an external spatialisation server.

## Concepts

**Four parts, always.** A spatial scene needs exactly four things, and a great deal of confusion comes from conflating them:

1. a **source**, usually mono: a sound file, a synthesiser, a live input;
2. a **layout**: where the loudspeakers physically are, as 2D or 3D coordinates;
3. a **trajectory**: where the source should be, over time;
4. an **algorithm**: given the layout and the position, how much signal each speaker gets.

A fifth object then does the actual audio work: a **spatialisation matrix** takes the source and the per-speaker weights the algorithm produced, and outputs the multichannel signal.

**Describing a layout.** Positions can come from a multi-cursor object whose points you place by hand, or be generated by expression: a small array generator returning `[cos(2*pi*i/n), sin(2*pi*i/n)]` lays out any number of speakers on a circle, and an expression generator can return coordinates literally. A point view object draws them so you can see what you declared.

**Trajectories.** A **path generator** produces movement along a line, a circle, or a spiral, and loops it. A **2D spline** lets you draw a path by hand and is the right tool when the movement is composed rather than generated. Both output a position, which is exactly what the algorithm wants.

**DBAP against GBAP.** **Distance-based amplitude panning** computes levels from distance and copes with irregular layouts, speakers at different heights, and arrays from two to a hundred: it is the default choice. **Grid-based amplitude panning** suits regular grids and, usefully, cascades: one instance can spatialise at the level of a building and others within each room. **Vector-based panning** and **ambisonics** are available through Faust libraries and plug-ins rather than as built-in processes.

**Beyond the built-ins.** The Faust standard library provides a professional circular spatialiser, `sp.spat`, developed by Laurent Pottier, which assumes a ring of speakers around the listener; its speaker count is a number in the script you can edit and recompile. The abclib library, installable through the package manager, adds 2D ambisonics up to high orders with encoders, decoders, and geometric tools. Plug-in suites such as SPARTA, IEM, and Ambix can be hosted per Lesson 21, and *score* can act purely as a controller for an external server such as SpatGRIS over OSC.

**Monitoring without the rig.** Almost nobody authors in the room they will perform in. Two honest approaches: fold the multichannel output down to stereo for a rough check, or use a head-related transfer function plug-in to simulate the layout over headphones. Both are approximations, and both are better than authoring blind.

## Walkthrough: a sound that circles four speakers

{: .note }

1. **Switch to the nodal view.** This lesson lives there.
2. **Add the source.** Drop a mono sound file and set it to loop in its inspector, so you have continuous material to listen to.
3. **Declare the layout.** Add a multi-cursor object and place four points in a square, or generate them with an expression that returns four positions on a circle. Add a point view to see them.
4. **Add the trajectory.** A path generator, set to a circle. Its output is a position over time.
5. **Add the algorithm.** A DBAP process. Cable the layout into its layout inlet and the trajectory into its source inlet.
6. **Add the matrix.** A spatialisation matrix, with four outputs. Cable the DBAP output into its data inlet and the sound file's audio into its audio inlet.
7. **Play.** The sound should move. If your interface has four outputs, route them; if not, continue to the next step.
8. **Fold down to stereo** so you can hear it on headphones: sum the four channels into two, or insert a head-related transfer function plug-in configured for your layout. This is a check, not the deliverable.
9. **Change the layout, not the piece.** Move one speaker's coordinate and listen again. Nothing else in the patch changes, which is the point of separating layout from trajectory.
10. **Change the number of speakers.** Regenerate the layout with eight points instead of four and set the matrix to eight outputs. The trajectory and the algorithm are untouched.
11. **Automate the trajectory's parameters.** Right-click the path generator's rate or radius port and create an automation, so the movement evolves over the piece rather than circling forever at one speed.
12. **Write the layout down.** Speaker numbers, physical positions, and which output channel each corresponds to. This document is what makes the piece installable by somebody else.

## Authoring for a room you are not in

Three habits that make the difference between a piece that transfers to the venue and one that has to be rebuilt there.

**Parameterise the layout.** If the speaker positions are generated from a count and a radius, changing venue is changing two numbers. If they are twelve hand-placed points, changing venue is an afternoon.

**Compose trajectories in normalised space.** A path expressed relative to the layout's extent survives a bigger or smaller room; one expressed in metres does not.

**Decide what happens with fewer speakers.** Every piece with a specific layout should have a documented answer for the venue that has half of it: which speakers are essential, and what the fold-down is. Deciding this while authoring is far cheaper than deciding it during a load-in, and DBAP's tolerance of irregular layouts is what makes a graceful answer possible.

## Object-based, channel-based, and controller

Three ways to organise a spatial project, and knowing which one you are in prevents most confusion.

**Object-based**, which this lesson builds: sources have positions, an algorithm computes speaker levels, and the layout is data. It adapts to any room, which is why it is the right default for touring work.

**Channel-based**: the material is already multichannel, mixed for a specific layout, and *score*'s job is to route it. Simple, robust, and tied to the layout it was mixed for. Ambisonic material is a variant, needing a decoder for the target layout.

**Controller**: the spatialisation happens in another application, a dedicated server such as SpatGRIS, and *score* sends it positions over OSC. Right when the venue already runs that server, or when the piece needs an algorithm *score* does not have.

These combine: a piece can have object-based sources, a channel-based bed, and a controlled external layer at once. What causes trouble is doing the same job twice, spatialising a source that is already positioned by the server, which produces movement nobody can explain.

## Common mistakes

- **Conflating trajectory and layout.** They are separate inputs to the algorithm, and keeping them separate is what makes the piece portable.
- **Forgetting the matrix.** DBAP produces weights, not audio. Without the matrix nothing is spatialised.
- **Expecting the algorithm to know your channel order.** Speaker one in your layout is whichever output you cabled it to. Write it down.
- **Authoring on stereo speakers with no fold-down**, then discovering in the room that the movement is inaudible.
- **Choosing ambisonics because it sounds more advanced.** For an irregular room, DBAP will usually do a better job with less machinery.
- **A trajectory in absolute units.** It will not survive the next room.
- **Not looping the source** while tuning, so you spend the session restarting playback.

## Exercise

Build the four-speaker circling scene, then change it three ways without touching the trajectory: to eight speakers on a circle, to four speakers in an irregular arrangement with one much further away, and to a layout with two speakers raised above the others. Listen to each through your fold-down and write one sentence on what changed perceptually.

**Success criterion:** all three variations work with the trajectory and algorithm untouched, and your written layout document is complete enough that someone else could patch your outputs. If a variation required editing the trajectory, your layout and movement are still entangled.
