# Eduardo Meneses — Writing Style Profile

*Generated from 21 documents spanning 2016–2025 (conference papers, journal articles, grant applications, cover letters, peer reviews, book chapters, institutional presentations — in EN, FR, PT).*

---

## Summary Profile

Eduardo Meneses writes with a precise, technically grounded voice that is simultaneously accessible and scholarly. His English prose is calm and methodical — claims are introduced with verbs of showing or arguing rather than asserting, and evidence is always anchored in cited literature. He is a practitioner-scholar who never lets technical complexity become opacity: he consistently restates domain jargon with parenthetical clarifications. His French, used in collaborative institutional contexts, becomes more elaborate and theory-heavy, favoring long embedded subordinates and dense citation chains. His Portuguese — now largely archival — is notably more personal, warmer in tone, and peppered with anglicisms that show the language of his specialist discourse bleeding through.

---

## 1. Voice and Tone

**Register:** Formal-to-semi-formal. Avoids both clinical stiffness and casual informality. In academic papers he is authoritative but never arrogant; in cover letters and bios he becomes collaborative and self-aware.

**Personal vs. impersonal:** First person used freely ("I led the technical and research aspects," "We then found a more practical solution") — consistent with NIME/ACM conference culture. In institutional documents shifts to "we" even when describing solo work.

**Authoritative vs. collaborative:** Both simultaneously. States findings with confidence but routinely shares credit, names collaborators by full role, and frames contributions as "opening discussions" rather than closing debates.

---

## 2. Sentence Structure

**Average length:** Medium to long — 25–45 words, often with 2–3 internal clauses. Short single-clause declaratives appear only as paragraph openers.

**Preferred constructions:**
- Compound structures joined by semicolons for parallel logical steps
- Appositive insertion mid-sentence
- Subordination with "while," "although," "however," "conversely"
- Parentheticals mid-sentence for examples, definitions, acronym expansions

**Active vs. passive:** Predominantly active (~85%). Passive used selectively — for mechanism descriptions or formal statements, never to hide the agent.

---

## 3. Paragraph Structure

**Openings:** Direct topic sentence — a claim, a result, or a contextualizing statement. Rarely opens with a question.

**Closings:** (a) a consequence or implication, (b) a forward reference to another section, or (c) a summary rephrasing of the claim.

**Length:** 3–6 sentences in academic prose; 2–3 in grant/institutional writing.

**Transitions:** Explicit logical connectors carrying precise weight:
- Use: "Furthermore," "Moreover," "However," "In contrast," "Additionally," "Conversely," "Simultaneously," "In other words," "Nevertheless"
- Avoid: "Also," "Plus," "Besides," "On top of that"

---

## 4. Vocabulary

**Technical jargon:** High, but consistently double-defined on first appearance. Acronyms always expanded: "Digital Musical Instruments (DMIs)," "Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU)."

**Preferred register:** Latin-derived academic vocabulary used naturally, not decoratively. "Facilitate," "affordance," "operandi," "paradigm," "longevity" appear naturally.

**Hedging:** Moderate and purposeful — "can vary," "may reduce," "is likely to," "we believe that" — used when genuinely uncertain, not as a stylistic tic.

**Intensifiers:** Sparse. No "very," "extremely," "incredibly." Precise superlatives only: "most significant," "particularly important."

**Plain vs. academic balance:** 70% academic, 30% plain. Prefers "use" over "utilize," "shows" over "demonstrates" in descriptive contexts.

---

## 5. Argumentation Style

**Claim introduction:** Maps the landscape of existing work first ("Several authors address X, without consensus"), then narrows to the specific contribution.

**Evidence presentation:** Inline citations, paraphrase and synthesis over direct quotation. Experimental results reported in past tense active before interpretation.

**Conclusions:** Drawn incrementally — partial conclusions close each section, synthesized in a final discussion. Conclusions are scoped precisely to evidence.

**Dialectical structure:** Uses contrast structurally, pairing two methods/types/models to illuminate each through comparison.

---

## 6. Rhetorical Devices

**Parallelism:** Very frequent. Both in lists and in paired within-sentence structures: "The cognitive aspect is determined by… The technical aspect is determined by… The artistic aspect is determined by…"

**Rhetorical questions:** Sparingly, to open a new angle: "But what if sound mixing… was no longer a technical necessity, but solely an intentionally creative sound design act?"

**Analogies and metaphors:** Rare in academic papers; present in institutional/conceptual pieces ("like an orchestral choreography," "as a site-specific orchestra"). The SAT beehive metaphor is a notable exception for promotional contexts.

**Enumeration:** Heavy and disciplined. Lists introduced by a colon after a full sentence.

**Anaphora:** Used in deliberate passages — interrogative lists establishing methodological checklists.

---

## 7. Idiosyncratic Patterns

- **Etymological grounding:** "The name 'X' comes from… and means…" on first appearance of a coined term.
- **Compound nominal phrase stacks:** "a twenty-year-old performance history," "research-creation projects involving arts, humanities, and exact sciences."
- **Layered disciplinary self-description:** "composer, electronic artist, music technologist, digital luthier, and performer" — role lists in descending prominence.
- **Problem-then-solution framing:** Even descriptive passages frame technological choices as solutions to stated problems.
- **"In the context of…":** Recurring framing phrase before any claim.
- **Careful credit attribution:** Collaborators named by full name, affiliation, and role — consistently and deliberately.
- **Feedback loops:** The word "feedback" appears in nearly every multi-year project description, in its design-process sense.

---

## 8. Multilingual Patterns

### English (primary)
- Most controlled and precise. Longest sentences, most subordination.
- First-person active is the norm; "I" and "we" both natural.
- Occasional trace Brazilianisms: "in the context of," "we could observe" (more literal than "we found").

### French (collaborative/institutional)
- Sentence length expands significantly — embedded relative clauses, citation chains at end of long paragraphs.
- More theoretical/philosophical referencing (Latour, Massumi, Dewey).
- More hedged: "il nous semble," "on peut envisager," "il nous paraît nécessaire."
- Inclusive neutral forms: "acteur·ices," "chercheur·ses," "spectateur·ices."
- First-person "je" almost absent; "nous"/"notre" dominate.

### Portuguese (archival/early)
- Most personal and warm register. Uses "meu mestrado," "obtive informações."
- Anglicisms embedded naturally: "live electronics," "feedback," "AMIs," "DMIs" — not translated.
- More paratactic than English: shorter sentences, less subordination.
- Code-switching to English increases in later PT documents (2022+) as academic voice matures in English.

## 10. Punctuation and Formatting Habits

- **Oxford comma:** Always — "arts, humanities, and exact sciences."
- **En/em dashes:** Do not use.
- **Semicolons:** Frequent, for parallel enumeration and logical sequences.
- **Italics:** Instrument names, foreign terms, concept-neologisms (*scenophony*, *roll*, *modus operandi*).
- **Bold:** Only for headers or key definitional terms.
- **Footnotes/endnotes:** Common for URLs, clarifications, and context that would disrupt paragraph flow.
- **Section numbering:** Explicit cross-references: "as discussed in section X," "can be found in section Y."

---

## 11. What He Avoids

- Passive voice as agency evasion ("it was found that," "it has been noted")
- Clichés: "game changer," "cutting-edge," "state-of-the-art" (except as field-standard terms)
- Unsupported superlatives: "the best," "uniquely revolutionary"
- Vague transitions: "also," "besides," "on top of that"
- Inflated vocabulary: "utilize" for "use," "leverage" for "use," "endeavor" for "try"
- Padding/throat-clearing: no "In today's world…" or "Technology has changed everything…" — enters the problem directly
- Rhetorical false modesty: states contributions clearly, without apologetic framing

## 12. Representative Example Sentences

1. "Since for DMIs the mappings between the gestural controller and sound synthesis are arbitrary, performers cannot rely on sound output to learn how to play a digital instrument." *(precise causality, two-clause)*

2. "The perception of an instrument as being more expressive (or more capable of allowing the performer to be expressive) can also impact its longevity." *(hedged, parenthetical expansion)*

3. "Composition and repertoire are impacted by music notation, repertoire impacts the development of instrumental technique, the lack of established instrumental technique impacts the development of DMI communities…" *(deliberate chain enumeration as rhetorical structure)*

4. "The name 'Puara' comes from the Old Tupi language and means 'to tie' or 'connect,' reflecting the framework's aim to bridge the gap between hardware and software in these creative domains." *(etymological grounding)*

5. "I believe I'm a good fit for the role of research director because of my commitment to maintaining and promoting free software and supporting the community of artists and researchers who explore the tools we develop." *(cover letter — direct, values-first)*

6. "But what if sound mixing — funnelling multiple discrete sources into fewer channels — was no longer a technical necessity, but solely an intentionally creative sound design act?" *(rhetorical question, em-dash apposition)*

7. "It is important to state that complex mappings are not a requirement for good DMI composition or performance." *(normative statement distinguishing position from common assumption)*

8. "Through its many activities, the SAT acts like a beehive where artists, audiences and the driving forces behind technological research interact collectively." *(metaphor — institutional/promotional register only)*

9. "These gestural descriptors were embedded starting with firmware version FW200422, shown in table X." *(hyper-precise version-dating as credibility signal)*

10. "The rapid evolution of embedded systems has opened exciting new possibilities for interactive art and creative technology." *(SIGGRAPH — rare promotional register, shorter, warmer, non-specialist audience)*

## 13. Style Recipe — Imitation Checklist

### Structure
- Open every paragraph with a direct topic sentence — never a vague scene-setter
- Cross-reference sections explicitly: "as discussed in section X"
- Close paragraphs with a consequence, a forward reference, or a synthesis

### Sentences
- Default length: 25–40 words, 2–3 clauses
- Active voice 85%. Reserve passive for mechanism descriptions
- Join parallel logical steps with semicolons rather than separate sentences

### Transitions
- Use: "Furthermore," "Moreover," "However," "In contrast," "Conversely," "Simultaneously," "In other words," "Additionally," "Nevertheless"
- Do not use: "Also," "Plus," "On top of this"

### Vocabulary
- Always expand acronyms in full on first use
- Use Latin-derived academic vocabulary naturally, not as decoration
- Hedge with "can," "may," "we believe," "suggest" only when genuinely uncertain
- Name collaborators by full name and institution when attributing credit

### Argumentation
- Map existing literature before placing your contribution in it
- Use contrast structurally: "Method A… In contrast, Method B…"
- State contributions at the end of sections; synthesize in the final discussion
- Scope conclusions precisely — do not claim more than the evidence supports

### Tone and values
- Distribute credit generously and specifically
- Unpack technical complexity — never use it to impress
- Frame tools as democratizing and community-enabling
- The practitioner's perspective (lab, performance, workshop) has equal weight with theory

### Formatting
- Oxford comma, always
- Italicize instrument names, neologisms, foreign terms
- Use footnotes for URLs and clarifying asides
- Use bold only for headers or key definitional terms
